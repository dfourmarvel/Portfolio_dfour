A paper cut and crushing chest pain do not wait in the same line

Aug 30, 2026 incident response from linkedin

A patient with a paper cut and a patient with crushing chest pain do not wait in the same line. Every emergency department decides that before either of them has met a doctor. That decision is called triage, and it runs on a structured framework — assess the presenting complaint, weigh it against a fixed set of discriminators, assign a category — not on who looks worse or who's shouting.

This week, in the Google Cybersecurity Certificate, I learned that a security operations centre runs on the identical logic, just aimed at alerts instead of patients. Receive and assess: is this a false positive, has it happened before, is it tied to a known vulnerability? Assign priority: weigh functional impact, information impact, and whether recovery is even possible if it turns out to be real. Only after both of those does an analyst actually start digging into logs and evidence.

Swap "patient" for "alert" and the process doesn't change — it just changes vocabulary. A failed login at 3am, from outside the network, is the security equivalent of a patient walking in short of breath: not automatically an emergency, but not something you wave through either. The framework exists precisely so that decision doesn't come down to instinct or how alarming something sounds in the moment.

Neither system is complicated. A handful of categories, a short list of questions. What's hard — in an ED and in a SOC — is running it correctly on the fortieth alert or the fortieth patient of a long shift, when the one question you're most tempted to skip is usually the one that mattered. Skip it in security and a real incident gets filed as noise. Skip it in medicine and "just a bit tired" turns out to be an epidural haematoma. The framework was never the hard part. Executing it perfectly, every time, is.
