The payroll transfer nobody sent

Jun 16, 2026 access control google cybersecurity certificate lab

A payroll transfer went to an unknown bank account. The finance manager didn't make a mistake. So who did?

That was the scenario in my latest Google Cybersecurity Certificate lab — a Payroll Incident Investigation that placed me as the first cybersecurity hire at a growing business. My job: trace the transaction, identify the threat actor, and close the gaps that made it all possible.

The investigation began with an event log. A payroll event was recorded on October 3, 2023, at 8:29:57 AM from IP address 152.207.255.255, under the account Legal\Administrator, on a machine flagged as Up2-NoGud. One cross-reference against the employee directory and the source came into focus — Robert Taylor Jr., a legal contractor whose end date was December 27, 2019. His IP matched the log exactly. A man whose contract had expired nearly four years prior had just initiated a fraudulent payroll transfer, because nobody had ever deactivated his account.

But the ghost account was only half the story. Pulling up the employee directory revealed something more systemic: every single person in the organization — full-time staff, part-time workers, seasonal employees, and external contractors alike — carried Admin authorization. A graphic designer. A seasonal sales associate. A manufacturer with no end date on file. All holding the same access level as the owner. The business hadn't just failed to offboard one contractor; it had built an environment where a breach from any direction was almost inevitable.

The takeaway is that access control is not a technical setting you configure once — it's an organizational discipline you enforce continuously. The Principle of Least Privilege and Role-Based Access Control exist precisely to prevent a single expired account from becoming an open door to financial systems. Pair those frameworks with strict offboarding protocols and MFA enforced enterprise-wide, and this incident never reaches the bank statement.

In medicine, every person who touches a patient's record must have a documented, role-specific reason for that access. When that discipline breaks down, people get harmed. Cybersecurity is no different — the only thing that changes is what ends up at risk.
