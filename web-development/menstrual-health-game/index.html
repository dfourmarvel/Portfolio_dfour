Menstrual Health Game

Snakes & Ladders reimagined to teach menstrual health — breaking stigma one board square at a time. Cross-listed under medicine, because it belongs in both.

Overview

The same board, pointed at a harder subject. Players roll onto a 100-square Snakes & Ladders board; ladders and snakes trigger menstrual health questions, right answers climb, wrong answers slide. The game is the delivery mechanism — the question bank is the actual content.

The problem

Menstrual health remains one of the most stigmatised and misunderstood topics in Ghana and beyond. Misinformation, missing resources and cultural silence carry a real cost in health and quality of life. The silence is the mechanism: a topic nobody will discuss directly is a topic where myths go uncorrected.

A game sidesteps that. Nobody has to volunteer to attend a menstrual health talk — they sit down to play Snakes & Ladders, and the education arrives in the middle of it. The project is aligned with the UN Sustainable Development Goals, specifically SDG 3 (Good Health & Well-being) and SDG 5 (Gender Equality).

The question bank

This is where a game about health either earns trust or loses it, so the bank was not written alone. Dr. Frank's expertise and guidance shaped both the questions and the educational direction of the project. It spans four areas:

  • Core true/false questions on menstrual health concepts, myths and facts.
  • Family, education & community — the social side, where most of the stigma actually lives.
  • WASH & period poverty — water, sanitation, hygiene, and access.
  • Clinical & public health — MCQ/SBA questions at a genuinely clinical level.

Every question is presented as both a true/false statement and a multiple-choice item, each with an explanation attached — because a right answer with no explanation teaches nothing, and a wrong answer with no explanation teaches less.

How it's built

Vanilla HTML, CSS and JavaScript — no framework, no build step, open index.html and play. It shares its engine with the Bible Quiz: the same board mechanics, AI players for solo play, audio, and read-aloud support for accessibility. Playwright covers the tests. Made in Ghana, for Ghana first.

what I learned

This is the project I am most glad exists. Not the most technically ambitious thing I have built — that's MedMatch — but the one where the gap between "a thing I coded" and "a thing that might change what someone believes about their own body" is smallest.

It also taught me the limits of my own expertise, which was a useful thing to run into early. I can build the board, write the engine, and ship it. I could not have written the clinical questions responsibly on my own, and the honest move was to bring in Dr. Frank rather than approximate it. A health game that is confidently wrong does more damage than one that never launched. Knowing which part of a project isn't yours to write turns out to be a skill of its own.
